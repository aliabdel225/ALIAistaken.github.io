Security Event Monitoring & Brute-Force Detection Using Splunk

A first-person walkthrough of my original 2021 Splunk lab, showing what I investigated, why I examined each result, and what my screenshots actually demonstrate.

01 / Why I built this project

In this lab, I used Splunk Enterprise to investigate security-related data and examine how monitoring rules can help identify suspicious activity. I wanted to understand how an analyst moves from raw search results to a meaningful detection, rather than treating every unusual event as proof of an attack.

My work covered four connected tasks: reviewing network traffic fields, examining a vulnerability-check alert, assessing event severity, and documenting a failed-login threshold associated with a brute-force alert. I completed the original exercise in 2021; this write-up explains my approach using the evidence I retained.

02 / My lab environment and approach

I worked in the Splunk Enterprise web interface, using its Search & Reporting view and alert-detail pages. My surviving screenshots show searchable events, summarized severity results, and two named alerts.

I do not have the original index inventory, complete data-source configuration, Splunk version, or full SPL queries in the 2021 report. I therefore describe the steps visible in my evidence rather than claiming a specific ingestion pipeline or reproducing commands I cannot verify.

03 / Step 1 — Investigating network activity

I began in Search & Reporting, where I inspected a results table containing timestamps, IP addresses, and upload/download measurements. My goal was to learn how to use these fields together: the timestamp establishes when activity occurred, the IP helps associate it with an endpoint, and traffic volumes provide a starting point for spotting unusual transfers.

I would compare the listed rows for repeated endpoints, time clustering, and unexpectedly large transfer volumes before deciding whether a particular connection deserves investigation. In my original answer I recorded “2020-02-20 at 14:21” and “It took 5 days.” Those are the values I documented, but the assignment question is missing, so I cannot confidently label them as an attack start time or dwell time.

04 / Step 2 — Reviewing a vulnerability-check alert

Next, I opened a Splunk alert named “vulnerability check.” I used the alert-detail page to understand how a saved detection is represented in the SIEM and where an analyst would inspect its status and triggering behavior.

My reasoning was that an alert name is only a starting point. To validate a vulnerability-related detection, I would need the underlying search, the matching event, the affected asset, and evidence of the actual exposure. 05 / Step 3 — Assessing event severity

I also examined a Splunk search view summarizing events by severity. This gave me a way to think about triage: severity labels help me decide which records to inspect first, but I should still examine the underlying events and surrounding context.

I would prioritize higher-severity results for review, then check their source, timestamp, affected system, and relationship to other activity. I would not automatically call a critical-labeled event a confirmed incident without supporting evidence.

06 / Step 4 — Understanding brute-force detection

I then reviewed a Splunk alert named “brute force attack.” In my original notes, I recorded “02/21/2020 at 2 am,” a normal baseline of 15 or fewer failed logins, and a threshold of 18 or more failed logins.

The important concept I demonstrated was baseline-informed alerting: if failed authentication attempts rise above the expected level, I have a reason to investigate potential password guessing. I would examine which account was targeted, whether failures came from one source or many, and whether a successful login followed the failures.

I cannot verify the aggregation window, actual SPL, source IP, targeted username, or whether this alert fired during the captured period. I therefore describe 18 as the documented threshold, not as proof of a confirmed brute-force attack.

07 / How I would validate the detection

If I were repeating this lab today, I would first define the authentication log source and a fixed time window. I would count failed logins per account and source IP, compare the counts with the baseline, and inspect nearby successful authentications. I would then simulate benign failed logins in an authorized test environment and confirm whether the alert triggers at the intended count.

For example, I would document the search logic, alert schedule, threshold, and test evidence. I would also check false positives such as forgotten passwords or services using outdated credentials. These are proposed validation steps, not activities proven by the 2021 screenshots.

08 / What I found and what the evidence supports

My evidence shows that I worked with Splunk search results containing traffic-related fields, reviewed severity-grouped events, and examined alerts labeled “vulnerability check” and “brute force attack.” My written answers preserve the two February 2020 timestamps, a five-day duration, and the failed-login baseline and threshold.

The original material does not prove a successful compromise, confirmed vulnerability, data exfiltration, or validated alert execution. Keeping that distinction clear is part of good incident reporting: observations, hypotheses, and conclusions should never be interchangeable.

09 / What this project taught me

This exercise helped me understand the connection between telemetry, investigative context, and alerting. I practiced reading Splunk results, interpreting alert screens, thinking in terms of normal-versus-abnormal behavior, and identifying what additional evidence an analyst needs before escalating a finding.

It also showed me how to improve my documentation. In a current investigation I would preserve the exact SPL, data-source details, time window, event samples, screenshots of the alert firing, and a concise incident disposition. That would make my analysis repeatable and easier for another analyst to audit.
